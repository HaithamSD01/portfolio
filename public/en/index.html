Smartovate Ltd

Intern, AI & cloud engineeringLondon, United Kingdom (remote)1 July – 31 August 2026

A multi-agent system, from specification to production

The problem

A single language model that produces and checks its own work lets its own mistakes through. Smartovate wanted a system where several agents split the work and check each other, deployed for real rather than left in a notebook.

My role

Solo on the project, supervised by Dr. Abdelkhalek Bakkari (CEO). The specification came from the company: 18 tasks over four two-week sprints. Architecture, code, tests, security and deployment were mine.

Results

  • 18 out of 18 specification tasks delivered. Every closed ticket in Jira carries its technical evidence (log, screenshot, commit).
  • 100% line coverage: 269 of 269 statements, 63 tests, measured with pytest-cov across the five application packages. I started from 38%; the contractual target was 80%.
  • 47.3 s median latency in production over five reference tasks (required threshold: 60 s). 15 successful runs out of 15, across three campaigns.
  • 4 sprints out of 4 closed early, up to 11 days ahead of the deadline.

How it is built

  • A four-agent hub-and-spoke topology: an Orchestrator that breaks the request down, a Coder that produces, a Reviewer that reads it over, and a Critic that validates and filters personal data.
  • The next speaker is picked dynamically (SelectorGroupChat). Two stop conditions prevent infinite loops: the TERMINATE keyword and a 15-message cap.
  • A FastAPI API protected by an API key, session memory on Azure Managed Redis and an audit log on Cosmos DB, all in a Docker container on Azure Container Apps. The 11 secrets go through secretRef; none is stored in plain text.
  • The coverage scope is pinned in a versioned .coveragerc: the Streamlit interface and the manual connection script are excluded, and anyone can reproduce the number by cloning the repository.

What went wrong, and what I did about it

  • I exposed my API key twice: once in a terminal, once in a screenshot. Rotated immediately both times. Since then, the .env tab gets closed before any screenshot.
  • Microsoft retired Azure Cache for Redis in the middle of sprint 3. Because memory sat behind an abstraction (dependency inversion), moving to Azure Managed Redis only took an environment variable change, with no change to application code.
  • A fake bug cost me 40 minutes: the error message came from a server started two hours earlier and never restarted. Lesson kept: check what the program actually reads, not what I think I wrote.
  • Four gaps with the specification are owned and justified in the report: no human-in-the-loop agent, only the Group Chat pattern implemented, no explicit 8,000-token context truncation, and LangChain and Azure Functions left out.
  • Code execution still runs in demo mode: the planned Docker isolation is impossible on Azure Container Apps (no Docker-in-Docker). I documented the constraint before it turned into an incident; the fix I identified, Azure Container Apps Dynamic Sessions, is not integrated yet.

Promopharm S.A. (Hikma Pharmaceuticals group)

Intern, physico-chemical quality controlHad Soualem, Morocco13 July – 13 August 2026

One month in a pharmaceutical quality-control lab

The problem

You can't judge a medicine by looking at it. I wanted to see from the inside how a lab turns a measurement into a compliance decision, and what it takes for that decision to still hold up in front of an inspector years after the batch was made.

My role

First-year engineering immersion internship, supervised by Mr. Hicham Touil, lab supervisor. I rotated across raw materials, finished products, stability and chromatography: watching first, then practising under supervision.

Results

  • One analysis followed end to end: an HPLC assay with degradation-product testing, from weighing to the signed line in the logbook, through system suitability and the calculation.
  • An appearance non-conformity spotted during a visual check (foreign particles in an ointment). The only out-of-spec result I saw during the internship, and it came from an eye, not an instrument.
  • A 64-page report written as an audit: the medicine's journey through the site, the quality framework, a case study, observations and improvement ideas.
  • A proposal submitted to the lab's improvement-ideas scheme: a digital twin of the lab and a calculation assistant to check dilutions. A proposal, not something built.

What I practised

  • The lab's techniques: liquid (HPLC) and gas (GC) chromatography, thin-layer chromatography, UV-Visible spectrophotometry, Karl Fischer titration, dissolution and disintegration, pH and conductivity measurement.
  • The framework that gives measurements their value: Good Manufacturing Practice, European Pharmacopoeia, ICH Q1A(R2) for stability, ALCOA+ data integrity, out-of-specification and out-of-trend handling (OOS/OOT).
  • Beyond the lab: a visit to production (controlled-atmosphere areas, pressure cascade, packaging) and time in microbiology.

What I had to correct along the way

  • Several of my first notes were wrong. I thought a LIMS was in use; the lab manager confirmed it wasn't. I also mixed up stability studies with batch release.
  • Each mistake was fixed the same way: checking my notes against a document, or asking the right person. That is probably what I keep most from this internship.
  • What I take from it for my own field: in a regulated environment, value doesn't come from the algorithm first, but from the quality and availability of the data it is fed.

How I work

I come from two interests at once: technology and health. What drives me is the moment a digital tool goes from prototype to something a team actually uses, with all its security, traceability and regulatory constraints.

The pattern on this site comes from zellige: the eight-pointed star is born from two overlapping squares. Simple rules that, combined, give a rich structure. That is also how I see a good system, whether it is made of AI agents or lab procedures.

Three rules I keep

  • Check what the program actually readsNot what I think I wrote. Half of my bugs came from there.
  • Write down what didn't workMy Smartovate report has a whole section on gaps. It costs some ego and earns credibility.
  • Data must be traceableA lesson from the lab: a result is only worth something if you can find out who produced it, when, and with which instrument.
